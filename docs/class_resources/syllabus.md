## Description

This 1-credit freshman course introduces students to drone applications in civil and construction engineering. Students will learn foundational drone safety, flight procedures, basic surveying concepts, GIS workflows, mission planning, photogrammetry processing, and FAA Part 107 preparation. The course combines short lectures, hands-on labs, field activities, data-processing exercises, and a group final project.

Students will begin with small indoor drones to build confidence with controls and flight safety before progressing to larger mission-capable drones. The course emphasizes professional field habits, including checklists, careful preparation, safety awareness, teamwork, and responsible data collection.

By the end of the semester, students will understand how drones can be used for engineering documentation, mapping, inspection, measurement, and project communication.

## How the Course Runs

Three threads run through every week of the semester.

1. **Lots of flying.** Students fly in nearly every lab, first small indoor drones and then the larger class drones outdoors. Most labs run as two stations: half the lab time flies with TAs while the other half works with the instructor, then the groups swap.
2. **FAA Part 107, a little every week.** Each pre-class reading includes a short Part 107 topic (airspace, weather reports, sectional charts, regulations). Each lecture opens with a one-question Part 107 quiz on it, which is also the lecture attendance check. By the practice test at the end of the semester, students have seen every exam area several times.
3. **Surveying.** Each lecture ends with a short surveying topic and a worked problem, and the homework includes a problem like it. These build toward the surveying part of the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam.

A typical lecture:

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Part 107 question (attendance), then the answer |
| 5–40 | The week's topic |
| 40–50 | Surveying note and example problem |

### Winter Weather

Many labs are outdoors in January through March. **Assume you will be outside during lab and dress for it.** If it is only cold, we fly. If snow, wind, or rain makes flying unsafe, the lab moves indoors to the small drones, and any data the lab needs comes from an earlier class flight so the lab work still gets done. Weather changes are posted in the class Teams channel.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. Treat a drone as a **measurement instrument**, and state what a given data product can and
   cannot be used to decide.
2. Report a measurement together with its **uncertainty**, and identify where that uncertainty
   came from.
3. Distinguish **accuracy** from precision, and check a result against independent ground truth
   rather than trusting the software's number.
4. Choose a measurement method by weighing the **accuracy required against the effort it costs**,
   across pacing, hand measurement, published imagery, and a drone flight.
5. Plan and fly a mapping mission whose altitude, overlap, and camera angle match the accuracy the
   job actually requires.
6. Process imagery into standard products (orthomosaic, DSM, DTM, point cloud, 3D model) and judge
   whether the result is fit for the decision at hand.
7. Operate safely and legally under FAA rules, by checklist, as a matter of habit.
8. Communicate a measurement result, and its limitations, well enough that another engineer could
   defend a decision made from it.

## ABET Course Outcomes

The following outcomes are recorded in the wording required for ABET accreditation. They cover the
same material as the list above.

1. Explain common civil engineering uses for drones, including surveying, mapping, inspection, construction documentation, and environmental monitoring.
2. Describe basic FAA Part 107 rules and identify when a remote pilot certificate is required.
3. Demonstrate safe drone operation practices, including pre-flight preparation, situational awareness, and checklist use.
4. Operate mini drones in a controlled indoor environment using basic flight maneuvers.
5. Prepare and use equipment and procedure checklists for drone fieldwork.
6. Explain the difference between accuracy and precision in measurement tasks.
7. Use basic GIS and mapping tools, including Google Earth and QGIS, to support measurement and mapping workflows.
8. Plan a drone mapping mission using appropriate flight parameters, overlap, altitude, coverage, and site constraints.
9. Describe the basic principles of photogrammetry and drone data processing.
10. Compare different drone sensor types, including RGB cameras, LiDAR, thermal, multispectral, and radar-based systems.
11. Work in a team to plan, fly, process, and communicate results from a drone-based project.
12. Present drone project results clearly through written, visual, and oral communication.
13. Fundamental surveying concepts

### Part 107 Practice Test Policy

The Part 107 practice test is intended to help students prepare for the FAA Remote Pilot Certificate exam if they are seeking licensure. The course practice test will include 60 questions and a 2-hour time limit. The passing threshold is 70%, or 42 out of 60 questions.

The practice test will mirror the Part 107 exam by using proctored-style settings, including one question per page and access to the FAA supplement as a PDF resource.

Students will receive two automatic attempts during the assigned test window. After the first attempt, students may review missed questions to guide further study.

A third attempt requires a brief consultation with a TA. Before taking a third attempt, the student must complete corrections for the lowest score from the first two attempts.

Students who pass may complete corrections to improve their assignment score, subject to instructor guidelines. If a student takes a third attempt and still does not earn 70%, corrections on the third attempt may raise the score up to, but not above, 70%.

The purpose of this policy is to encourage preparation, allow targeted learning, and help students develop confidence before taking the real FAA exam.

## Late Work

Any assignment except a pre-class reading quiz may be turned in late. Pre-class reading quizzes cannot be made up, reopened, or redone for any reason.

* Late work loses **10% of the assignment's full points for each week it is late**: 1 to 7 days late is 10% off, 8 to 14 days is 20% off, and so on, up to a maximum of **50%** off.
* The penalty is taken from the points possible, not from the score earned. On a 20-point assignment turned in one week late, 2 points come off whatever the student earns.
* **No work is accepted after 11:59 PM on the last day of classes.** Until then, even work due in the first week can still be turned in, for at most 50% off.
* A late TRUST certificate still loses points, and the student cannot fly until it is turned in.

If an assignment is turned in late, it is the student's responsibility to contact the TA responsible for grading that assignment in order to have the submission rescored. Information about which TA grades each assignment will be posted on the course website.

## Attendance Policy

Attendance is required for both lectures and labs. Drone work depends on safety training, instructor supervision, equipment coordination, and group participation.

Lecture attendance is the opening Part 107 question in Canvas, open only during the first minutes of class. Lab attendance will be tracked using a lab sign-in sheet. Lab sign-ins are also important for safety and regulatory tracking.

Attendance is taken **at the start** of every lecture and lab; a student who arrives late misses it. In the two final project flight weeks, only the groups flying that week have lab, and attendance is taken at the start of their flight time. If neither partner shows up, the group reschedules its flight with a TA.

Attendance is recorded for every lecture and lab time. No absences are automatically dropped. Because many course activities involve safety training, group coordination, and hands-on flight work, students are expected to attend consistently. Missing class may affect the attendance portion of the course grade and may make it difficult to complete required lab activities.

**Most flying cannot be made up.** Students may only fly the class drones with a certified TA or instructor present, so a missed flying lab cannot be done alone later. Plan to attend every lab.

Because some flight activities depend on weather and equipment availability, students are expected to be flexible and responsive to schedule adjustments. If a student must miss a lab, they should contact the instructor or TA as early as possible.

### Lab Times

Each student is assigned one lab time (Lab A, B or C) and attends it every week. Switching lab times week to week is not allowed.

To attend a different lab time for **one week**, a student must tell the professor or the head TA **by the end of their own lab one week before**. The professor or head TA will decide whether the student can simply attend the other lab time or needs to find someone in that lab time to trade with for that week.

## Classroom Procedures

### Before Class

Students should complete assigned readings, including the week's short Part 107 topic, and pre-class quizzes before coming to class. Reading quizzes are usually designed as completion and preparation checks. All quizzes are open book and will allow only one attempt to encourage careful preparation before class.

### During Lecture

Most lectures will be discussion-based and centered around the assigned readings. Students are expected to come prepared to engage with the material, ask questions, and contribute to class conversations. Lectures will often focus on applying concepts, discussing real-world examples, clarifying difficult topics, and connecting readings to lab activities and engineering practice.

Students should bring a laptop or device when needed for mapping, GIS, mission planning, or test-prep activities. Students are expected to participate respectfully, take notes, ask questions, and stay engaged.

### During Labs

Students must follow all lab safety instructions. Students work in assigned pairs within their lab time; no one flies alone. Most labs open with a short stand-up from the instructor, then split into two stations that swap halfway: one flies with the TAs, the other works with the instructor. Flight labs may use sign-up windows to manage equipment and instructor supervision.

Students may only operate any drone used in this course, including mini drones, after earning their TRUST certificate. Before each flight, there will be a safety brief. Students must follow all safety instructions and TA or instructor guidance during the lab.

Except for the mini drones used in introductory indoor labs, all course drones may only be operated when a certified TA or instructor holding a current FAA Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate is present and supervising the flight operation. Only one larger drone may be airborne per certified TA or instructor at a time. This policy applies even if a student already holds their own Part 107 certificate. Students may not independently operate course drones outside of supervised course activities.

### Drone and Equipment Expectations

Students are expected to handle all course equipment carefully. This includes drones, batteries, controllers, chargers, SD cards, laptops, and other field equipment.

Students should follow all instructor and TA instructions for:

* Battery handling.
* Drone setup.
* Controller setup.
* Calibration.
* Site safety checks.
* Takeoff and landing.
* Data storage.
* Equipment return.

### Checklist Culture

A major theme of the course is that safe and reliable pilots operate by checklist. Students will practice creating, using, and improving checklists throughout the semester.

Checklists should include both:

1. **Equipment lists** — items required for the mission, such as charged batteries, SD cards, controllers, chargers, laptops, and field supplies.
2. **Procedure lists** — step-by-step actions before, during, and after flight.

Students will also use the [course flight checklists](flight_check_list/index.md) throughout the semester during labs and field activities. A printed copy is kept with every class drone.

## Study Habits

Success in this course depends on steady preparation rather than last-minute work. Students should develop habits that support both technical learning and safe field practice.

Recommended study habits include:

* Complete readings before class.
* Take short notes during lectures.
* Review missed quiz and test questions.
* Practice using checklists.
* Keep organized lab notes.
* Save copies of mission plans and project files.
* Review FAA Part 107 topics regularly.
* Ask for help early when software, data, or flight concepts are unclear.
* Meet with teammates outside of class when needed for project planning.

For the Part 107 practice test, students should treat the course exam as preparation for the real FAA exam. Study should include sectional charts, weather products, NOTAMs, airspace, runway numbering, airport layouts, regulations, and safe operating practices.

## Teaching Philosophy

This course is built around hands-on learning, curiosity, and professional habits. Drones are exciting tools, but they are also aircraft, data-collection systems, and engineering instruments. Students will be expected to approach drone work with both enthusiasm and responsibility.

The course uses a “think first, then refine” approach. For example, students may first create a checklist before being shown a professional example. This helps students identify what they already understand, discover what they missed, and appreciate why formal procedures matter.

Labs are designed to help students learn by doing. Mistakes (short of crashing the drones), questions, and revisions are part of the learning process, especially when students are working with new tools, software, and field procedures. At the same time, safety-related instructions must be followed carefully and immediately.

The goal of the course is not only to teach students how drones work, but also to help them think like responsible engineers: prepared, observant, collaborative, precise, and safety-minded.
