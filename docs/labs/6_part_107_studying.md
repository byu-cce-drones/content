# Part 107 Studying Lab

!!! abstract "Key Takeaways"
    - Everything you need to pass the Part 107 knowledge test is free, but it is spread across a
      dozen documents and websites. Knowing exactly where each one is turns "study for the test" into
      a list of things you can actually open.
    - Every answer on the test comes from somewhere you can point to: a regulation, a chapter of an
      FAA guide, or a figure in the testing supplement. Practise finding the source, not just the
      answer.
    - A figure question is worked the same way every time: find the figure, find the red number,
      read the symbol with the legend open, apply one rule, check the units.
    - A METAR is a weather report you will read before every real flight. Decoding today's report for
      the Provo airport is the same skill the test asks for, with real numbers.
    - A study plan that names a chapter, a figure or a video for each day gets done. "Study charts"
      does not.

!!! note "What today is, and is not"
    This lab is **not** a study session for the exam. It is a lab about **finding things**: by the
    end of it you will have opened every study resource the course recommends, proved you can find
    your way around each one, and used it to answer a question. When you study on your own next
    week, you will not be lost.

    A TA leads the session. You work in **pairs** and turn in one Resource Passport per pair.

## Background

The FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) **Part 107** knowledge test is the written exam for the
Remote Pilot Certificate, the licence that lets you fly a drone for work. The test is sixty
multiple-choice questions, and nearly every one of them is answered in a public FAA document: the
regulations themselves (Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, or **CFR**), the FAA's
plain-language guide to them (**Advisory Circular AC 107-2A**), the *Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical
Knowledge* (**PHAK**), and the **Airman Knowledge Testing Supplement** (FAA-CT-8080-2H), which holds
every chart and weather report the test refers to.

The course [Study Resources](../part_107_license/part_107_resources.md) page lists all of these and
ranks them. The problem is not a shortage of material; it is that a first-time reader does not know
where the legend is in a 113-page supplement, which chapter of a 104-page circular covers operations
over people, or how to get a real weather report off AviationWeather.gov. Students who do not know
these things study from whatever video comes up first, which is often out of date (anything before
April 2021 teaches the wrong night, over-people and Remote ID rules).

Today's lab fixes that. Five short activities take you into each resource with a specific thing to
find, and the worksheet you fill in, the **Resource Passport**, becomes your own index to the
material for the two weeks of study that follow. It is also practice for the habit the whole course is
built on: an answer is only worth something if you can say where it came from.

!!! warning "Sources, not memory"
    The one rule for the whole lab: **every answer needs a source** — the page, the section number,
    or the URL you found it at. In the look-it-up race a correct answer without its source scores
    half. On a real job, "I'm pretty sure the rule is..." is how a pilot ends up with a violation.

---

## Objectives

By completing this lab, students will:

1. Open each of the core Part 107 study resources and locate a specific fact in it.
2. Navigate the testing supplement: the sectional chart legend, the chart excerpts, and the weather
   figures.
3. Work a figure-based test question by the five-step method in the Knowledge Review.
4. Find the regulation or guidance that answers a rule question, and cite it by section or chapter.
5. Retrieve a live METAR for the Provo airport, decode it group by group, and decide whether a
   Part 107 flight could legally go.
6. Identify their own two weakest test areas and write a two-week study plan that names a specific
   resource for each day.

### Part 2 — Reading a Figure Question

About a fifth of the test is airspace and charts, and it is the most-missed area. Every figure
question is worked the same way; the steps are in
[How to Read a Figure Question](../part_107_license/part_107_knowledge_review.md#how-to-read-a-figure-question):

1. **Find the figure** in the supplement (chart excerpts start at Figure 20, PDF page 53).
2. **Find the area number** — the large red circled number — and look near it for the feature named.
3. **Use the legend** (PDF page 13). Do not guess what a symbol means.
4. **Apply one rule.** Nearly every figure question ends with a single regulation or definition.
5. **Check the units.** Chart altitudes are MSL; obstacle heights in parentheses are AGL; the answer
   choices will offer both.

The four questions below are from the FAA's own public sample set, quoted word for word. None of
them was worked in the Week 9 lecture, so the [slides](../slides/week_09.html) do not give these
away.

#### The TA works this one aloud

Watch for each of the five steps as the TA does them.

??? example "FAA sample question 9"
    (Refer to FAA-CT-8080-2H, Figure 20, area 3.) With ATC authorization, you are operating your
    small unmanned aircraft approximately 4 SM southeast of Elizabeth City Regional Airport (ECG).
    What hazard is indicated to be in that area?

    A. High density military operations in the vicinity.
    B. Unmarked balloon on a cable up to 3,008 feet AGL.
    C. Unmarked balloon on a cable up to 3,008 feet MSL.

#### Your pair works these three

For each, write in the Passport the **figure and area you used**, your **answer**, and **one
sentence on why**. Do not stop at the letter; the sentence is the part that transfers to the next
question.

??? example "FAA sample question 24"
    (Refer to FAA-CT-8080-2H, Figure 21.) What airport is located approximately 47 (degrees) 40
    (minutes) N latitude and 101 (degrees) 26 (minutes) W longitude?

    A. Mercer County Regional Airport.
    B. Semshenko Airport.
    C. Garrison Airport.

??? example "FAA sample question 26"
    (Refer to FAA-CT-8080-2H, Figure 12.) The wind direction and velocity at KJFK is from

    A. 180° true at 4 knots.
    B. 180° magnetic at 4 knots.
    C. 040° true at 18 knots.

??? example "FAA sample question 31"
    (Refer to FAA-CT-8080-2H, Figure 26.) What does the line of latitude at area 4 measure?

    A. The degrees of latitude east and west of the Prime Meridian.
    B. The degrees of latitude north and south of the equator.
    C. The degrees of latitude east and west of the line that passes through Greenwich, England.

!!! tip "Latitude and longitude on a sectional"
    The thick black lines with tick marks are the whole-degree lines of latitude (running east–west,
    labelled like **47°**) and longitude (running north–south, labelled like **101°**). Each small
    tick is one minute; the longer ticks mark 10 and 30 minutes. Latitude increases to the north;
    west longitude increases to the **west**, so 101°26′W is 26 minutes to the left of the 101° line.

When you have all three, compare notes with the pair next to you before the TA takes answers. The
answers, with explanations, are on the [answer page](6_part_107_studying_answers.md) — check them
**after** the lab.

### Part 3 — Look-It-Up Race

Swap driver and navigator. The TA runs this as a race between pairs on the board. Ten rule questions;
for each, write the **answer** and **where it is written**: the CFR section (for example
"14 CFR 107.51(c)"), the AC 107-2A chapter and page, or the URL.

**A correct answer without its source scores half.** The point of the race is the second column.

Where to look: the regulations are on [eCFR Part 107](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-107){target="_blank"}
(use the browser's find-in-page), with Remote ID in [Part 89](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-89){target="_blank"}
and registration in [Part 48](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-48){target="_blank"}.
The FAA's explanations are in [AC 107-2A](https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_107-2A.pdf){target="_blank"}:
chapter 5 is the operating rules, chapter 6 the certificate, chapter 7 maintenance, chapter 8
operations over people. Anything the regulations do not cover, the [Part 107 Exam](../part_107_license/faa_exam_planning_and_overview.md)
reading does, with its own source links.

    | 2 | Minimum distance below a cloud, and horizontally from it? |
    | 3 | How many hours after drinking alcohol before you may fly? |
    | 4 | Who submits a Declaration of Compliance, and for which categories of operation over people? |
    | 5 | A standard Remote ID aircraft stops broadcasting in flight. What must the person on the controls do? |

### Part 4 — Today's Real Weather

A **METAR** (Meteorological Aerodrome Report) is the hourly weather observation from an airport,
written in a fixed code so pilots anywhere can read it. The test gives you METARs to decode
(supplement Figure 12); real flights need you to decode a live one. Today you do it for **KPVU**,
Provo Municipal Airport, a few miles from campus.

1. Open the live report: [**aviationweather.gov/data/metar/?ids=KPVU**](https://aviationweather.gov/data/metar/?ids=KPVU){target="_blank"}.
   Copy the raw line (it starts `METAR KPVU` or `SPECI KPVU`) into the Passport, with the time you
   pulled it. A sample from the morning this page was written:

    ```text
    METAR KPVU 271356Z 12003KT 10SM CLR 05/02 A3008 RMK AO2 SLP173 T00500022
    ```

2. **Decode it group by group** in the Passport, without the decoded view: station, day and time
   (convert the Z time to local), wind, visibility, present weather, clouds, temperature and dew
   point, altimeter, remarks. [The Part 107 Exam §VI.B](../part_107_license/faa_exam_planning_and_overview.md#b-metars-and-tafs)
   explains each group.
3. **Check yourself** with the decoded view, the same URL with `&decoded=yes` added:
   [aviationweather.gov/data/metar/?ids=KPVU&decoded=yes](https://aviationweather.gov/data/metar/?ids=KPVU&decoded=yes){target="_blank"}.
   Fix anything you got wrong, and note what it was.
4. **Decide: could we fly under Part 107 right now?** Work through the checks on the Passport sheet:
    * **Visibility** at least 3 statute miles (§107.51(c)).
    * **Clouds.** The ceiling is the lowest **BKN** or **OVC** layer, in hundreds of feet AGL
      (`OVC007` is 700 ft; `CLR` means none). You must stay 500 ft below any cloud (§107.51(d)), so
      a flight at 400 ft AGL needs a ceiling of at least 900 ft. A lower ceiling does not forbid the
      flight, it lowers your maximum altitude; say what it would be.
    * **Wind** against the aircraft's limit. The limit is in the aircraft's manual; your TA will
      give the number for the class aircraft. Remember the METAR wind is in **knots** and is a
      **true** direction.
    * **TFRs.** Check [tfr.faa.gov](https://tfr.faa.gov/){target="_blank"} for anything over Provo.
      On a home football game day the stadium restriction closes campus; see Part 3, question 10.
    * **Airspace.** KPVU sits in **Class D** airspace, which covers all of campus. Weather that is
      legal is not enough: a real flight here would also need ATC authorization through **LAANC**
      (§107.41). Write down what the request would be.
5. Write **GO** or **NO-GO** and a one-sentence reason. The TA projects one pair's decode and asks
   each pair for its call.

!!! tip "Why this is worth fifteen minutes"
    Decoding ten real METARs is day 6 of the [two-week study plan](../part_107_license/part_107_knowledge_review.md#two-week-study-plan).
    This is the first one. The others take two minutes each once you have done this one.

### Part 5 — My Study Plan

The last part is individual: each of you fills in your own plan on the Passport's last sheet (one
pair, one file; put both plans in, one below the other, or each partner uploads their own copy with
the plan filled in).

1. Look at the test areas and their weights on the [Drill by topic](../part_107_license/part_107_resources.md#drill-by-topic)
   table: Regulations 48 %, Operations 25 %, Airspace and charts 20 %, Weather 5 %, Loading 2 %.
   Pick your **two weakest** — the two you would least like to be tested on tomorrow. Be honest;
   the plan is for you.
2. For each, write the resource the table names for it. That is where you start.
3. Fill in fourteen days. Use the [two-week study plan](../part_107_license/part_107_knowledge_review.md#two-week-study-plan)
   as the skeleton and the [ranked list](../part_107_license/part_107_resources.md#the-top-8-ranked)
   for how long each item takes. Every row names a **specific** resource: "AC 107-2A chapter 8",
   "supplement Figures 20–23 with the legend", "Mr. Mig, Load Factor (7 min)". Give your weakest two
   areas extra days.
4. Put real dates in the Date column, working back from your test date if you have one, or from the
   graded practice exam's closing date in Learning Suite if you do not.

The TA checks each plan for one thing: that every day names a resource you could open right now.
"Study charts" is sent back.

## Looking Ahead

Next: [Final Project Overview](../final_project/overview.md).

This is the last stand-alone lab. From Week 10 the Thursday sessions belong to the **final
project**: your team plans a flight, flies it, processes the data, and defends a measurement from
it. Everything today was about finding the rule and citing the source; the project asks the same of
your data. A measurement is only worth something if you can say where it came from, how good it is,
and what decision it will and will not support.

The certificate is a longer road. Two weeks of the plan you wrote today, the graded practice exam,
and then, if you choose, the real test at a PSI center. Many students in this course take it; the
[Part 107 Exam](../part_107_license/faa_exam_planning_and_overview.md#b-getting-your-certificate-step-by-step)
reading walks through scheduling it.
